The price increase formula

Monthly revenue before the change is your current price times your paying customers:

revenue before = current price × customers

After the change, only the customers who stay pay, and they pay the new price:

revenue after = new price × customers × (1 - share who leave)

Break-even churn is the share who can leave while revenue stays the same. Set the two lines equal and solve:

break-even churn = 1 - current price / new price = (new price - current price) / new price

If the new price is lower than the current one, there is no churn to absorb. Instead you need more customers to keep the same revenue: customers needed = customers × current price / new price.

A worked example

With the defaults above, 400 customers pay $50 a month, so revenue before is 400 × $50 = $20,000 a month. The price goes to $60, a 20% increase.

Break-even churn is ($60 - $50) / $60 = 16.67%, so you could lose up to 66 of your 400 customers and still earn the same. If 10% leave, 360 stay and pay $60, so the new monthly revenue is 360 × $60 = $21,600, which is $1,600 more than before. If 20% left instead, 320 would stay and revenue would fall to $19,200.

Note that break-even churn (16.67%) is below the price increase (20%), because the customers who stay pay the higher price.

What this number misses

The calculator only looks at the customers you already have. A price change also moves how many new visitors become paying customers, and that rarely moves the same way across channels. Buyers from search or a referral often arrived wanting the product and may convert about as well at the new price, while buyers from a deal site or a discount-driven campaign may be the first to drop off. A blended conversion rate can stay flat while one channel quietly stops paying for itself.

So after a price change, watch new paying customers and revenue by channel, not just churn. You can do that by hand with the churn rate calculator and your signups per source, or let Virling do it from your payments: it credits each payment to the channel, referrer, campaign and landing page of the visit that brought it, so you can compare each channel before and after the change.

How to try a price increase safely

  1. Charge new customers first. Keep current customers on the old price while you learn how many visitors still buy. Nobody who already pays can leave over a price they are not charged.
  2. Write down the period before. Note visitors, new customers and revenue from new customers for each channel, and your normal monthly churn from the churn rate calculator.
  3. Set the stopping point first. The break-even share also works for new buyers: from $50 to $60, the share of visitors who pay can fall by 16.67%, say from 3% to 2.5%, before you earn less from the same visitors. Decide the number, and how long you will wait, before you change anything.
  4. Wait for enough buyers. A few sales either way can be chance. Put both periods into the A/B test calculator as a rough check; a before and after comparison can also move with the season or the traffic mix.
  5. Compare channels before and after. In Virling, set a Custom range for the days since the change and use Compare to other dates for a period of the same length before it. Click a channel to filter the page, and read New customers against Visitors. Revenue in a range also includes renewals, which keep the channel of the first payment, so do not judge new buyers by revenue alone. See how to read the dashboard.
  6. Then move current customers in groups. Tell them the date in advance, start with a small group, and compare its churn with the customers still on the old price. Stop if the extra share who leave reaches the break-even churn for your increase.

Price increase calculator FAQ

How do I calculate how many customers I can lose after a price increase?

Divide the price increase by the new price. Going from $50 to $60 is a $10 increase on a $60 price, so you can lose 10 / 60 = 16.67% of paying customers and still earn the same revenue. Lose fewer and revenue goes up; lose more and it goes down.

What is break-even churn?

It is the share of current customers who can leave after a price change while revenue stays exactly where it was. The formula is 1 - old price / new price, which is the same as the price increase divided by the new price.

Why is break-even churn smaller than the price increase percentage?

Because the customers who stay pay the new, higher price. A 20% increase from $50 to $60 breaks even at 16.67% churn, not 20%, since each remaining customer now brings $60 instead of $50.

Should existing customers keep the old price?

That is a business choice. If you keep current customers on the old price, this calculator does not apply to them, and the change only affects new customers. Then the number to watch is how many visitors still convert at the new price.

Does this work for annual plans or one-time prices?

Yes, as long as the old and new prices cover the same period. Enter annual prices to get annual revenue. For one-time purchases, use buyers per month instead of paying customers.

How far can conversion fall after a price increase?

By the same share as break-even churn. From $50 to $60 that is 16.67%, so if 3% of visitors paid before, 2.5% paying now brings the same first-payment revenue from the same number of visitors. Below that, new customers bring you less.

How long should I wait before judging a price increase?

For new customers, longer than they usually take from first visit to payment. For current customers, at least one full billing cycle, since a monthly customer meets the new price at the next renewal and an annual customer only when the year ends.
