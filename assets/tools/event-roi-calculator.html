The event ROI formula

Cost is everything you spent to be there, including time. Value is the leads you met, times the share that buy, times what one customer is worth:

total cost = booth or ticket + travel + staff days × cost per staff day

cost per lead = total cost / leads

customers = leads × share that become customers

customers to break even = total cost / value per customer, rounded up

ROI = (customers × value per customer - total cost) / total cost

An ROI of 0% means the event exactly paid for itself, and below 0% it lost money. Value per customer works best as gross profit: what a customer pays you, less what it costs to serve them. If you enter revenue instead, the result is return on revenue and will look higher.

A worked example

These are made-up numbers, the same ones the calculator starts with, not data from a real business. A booth costs $3,000 and travel $1,200. Two people spend three days each, so 6 staff days at $300 is $1,800. The total cost is $3,000 + $1,200 + $1,800 = $6,000.

You come home with 80 leads, so a lead cost $6,000 / 80 = $75. If 5% become customers, that is 80 × 5% = 4 customers. At $2,500 each they are worth $10,000, and ROI is ($10,000 - $6,000) / $6,000 = 66.7%. Breaking even takes $6,000 / $2,500 = 2.4, so 3 customers.

Leave out the staff days and the cost drops to $4,200, and the same 4 customers look like an ROI of 138.1%. Nothing about the event changed, only the cost that was counted.

Count customers who buy months later

Few people pay at a booth. They go home, talk to their team, try the product and pay weeks or months later. Two things decide whether that payment is still counted for the event: a tag that says where the visit came from, and a window long enough to reach back to it.

Give each event its own QR code or short link

A phone camera opens a QR code with no referrer, so an untagged code counts as Direct. The QR code generator with UTM tags puts the tags in the code. Use one campaign per event and placement, such as event-spring-expo-2027-booth, and never reuse last year's code. For a slide or a badge, a short link is easier to type: a tracked link in Virling adds the tags on each click.

Point both at a page on your site, not straight at a payment link, so the visit is recorded. Tag the follow-up email with the event's name too. Many people scan on a phone and buy on a laptop, and a visit on one does not match a payment on the other by itself. The email gives them a tagged visit on the device they buy on. Use a window longer than your sales cycle

An attribution window is the number of days before a payment in which a visit can earn credit. A visit older than the window is ignored, so a customer who buys after it closes is not counted for the event. Virling uses 90 days by default and offers 30, 60, 90 or 365. How to choose an attribution window explains how to base it on your own time to pay.

Take the same made-up event, with its cost of $6,000. Four people scan the booth code on the day, and later come back only by typing the address, in the same browser. They pay 18, 55, 120 and 174 days after the event, and each is worth $2,500. This is what each window counts:

Same event, same customers. At 90 days it looks like a loss, because two of the four had not paid yet. Only the longest window counts all of them, and only once 174 days have passed.

How to use the result

  • Before you book: work out the customers to break even, then divide by the share of leads that usually buy. In the example, 3 customers at 5% means 60 leads. If you cannot picture meeting that many people, the event is too expensive at that price.
  • After the event: replace the guessed leads with the real count. Compare cost per lead with your other channels, but do not stop there, since a cheap lead who never buys is worth nothing.
  • When your sales cycle has passed: replace the share that become customers with the real one, and compare events on ROI.

Event ROI calculator FAQ

How do you calculate event ROI?

Add the booth or ticket, travel and staff time to get the total cost. Multiply leads by the share that become customers, then by what one customer is worth. Subtract the cost from that value and divide by the cost. In the example on this page, $10,000 of value on $6,000 of cost is an ROI of 66.7%.

What costs should I include in trade show ROI?

The booth or ticket, anything you pay to fit it out, shipping, travel, hotels and food, and the working days of everyone who prepares, travels and follows up. Staff time is easy to leave out because no invoice arrives for it, and leaving it out makes the ROI look better than it is.

How many customers does an event need to break even?

Divide the total cost by the value of one customer and round up, since you cannot win part of a customer. An event that cost $6,000 with customers worth $2,500 each needs 2.4, so 3 customers.

What is a good ROI for a trade show?

Above 0% means the event returned more than it cost. Beyond that it depends on your margin and on how long your deals take, so compare it with your other events and channels, worked out with the same formula over the same number of days.

How long should I wait before working out event ROI?

At least as long as your customers usually take from first contact to payment. Work out a first figure soon after the event if you like, label it as early, and run the numbers again once that time has passed.

How do I know which customers came from an event?

Give every event its own tagged QR code or short link that opens a page on your site, put a tag on the follow-up email too, and use a tool that joins each payment to the visits before it. Keep the lead list as well, because someone who never used a tagged link cannot be found by any tag.
