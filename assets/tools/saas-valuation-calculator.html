The valuation formula

A revenue multiple valuation is ARR times a multiple. With a low and a high multiple you get a range:

valuation = ARR × multiple

If you enter MRR, ARR is MRR times 12. ARR in 12 months applies your yearly growth rate once, and the forward range uses the same two multiples on that figure:

ARR in 12 months = ARR × (1 + growth rate)

Monthly churn compounds over a year. The share of today's revenue lost in 12 months, before any new sales or expansion, is:

yearly churn = 1 - (1 - monthly churn)12

The calculator does not pick a multiple for you. Multiples depend on the buyer, the market at the time, growth, retention, margins and deal terms, and no single table fits every business. Enter the multiples you have from offers, advisors or comparable deals you trust, and use the range to see what they imply.

A worked example

With the defaults above, MRR is $50,000, so ARR is $50,000 × 12 = $600,000. At 3× and 6× the implied valuation is $1,800,000 to $3,600,000.

ARR grew 40% over the last year. If the next year matches, ARR in 12 months is $600,000 × 1.4 = $840,000, and the same multiples give $2,520,000 to $5,040,000.

Churn of 2% a month means 1 - 0.9812, about 21.5% of today's revenue, is gone within a year unless it is replaced. That is the part of the business a buyer will look at hardest.

When the valuation misleads

The multiple magnifies every mistake in the ARR below it. The result also leaves out profit, cash and debt, and it is wrong when the multiple you borrowed was paid on a different kind of revenue, such as ARR in 12 months or all revenue. Here is a made-up example, step by step, with the default test multiples of 3× and 6× and the $30,000 month from the run rate calculator page: $14,000 of monthly subscriptions, $12,000 of yearly plans paid up front and $4,000 of setup fees.

  1. Enter the whole month as MRR. $30,000 × 12 = $360,000, which gives $1,080,000 to $2,160,000.
  2. Take out the setup fees. $26,000 × 12 = $312,000, which gives $936,000 to $1,872,000.
  3. Spread the yearly plans over 12 months. $12,000 / 12 = $1,000, so MRR is $14,000 + $1,000 = $15,000 and ARR is $180,000. That gives $540,000 to $1,080,000, half of step 1. This company has sold no other yearly plans.
  4. Look at the forward range. At 40% growth, ARR in 12 months is $180,000 × 1.4 = $252,000, which gives $756,000 to $1,512,000 for a year that has not happened yet.
  5. Check what that year takes. At 2% churn a month, about 21.5% of $180,000, or about $38,750, is lost. Reaching $252,000 takes about $72,000 + $38,750 = $110,750 of new and expansion ARR, and more if new customers cancel too.

So before you enter a figure, get ARR from the recurring part of the run rate. If the company is burning cash, the burn multiple calculator takes the change in that ARR and shows what the growth costs, which the growth rate here leaves out.

Where revenue comes from matters

Two companies with the same ARR and the same multiple on paper can be worth very different amounts to a buyer. A buyer looks at how durable the revenue is: how much existing customers keep, expand or cancel, and whether the channels that bring new customers will keep working after the sale. Revenue that comes from one channel, or from customers who leave within a few months, gets discounted. Revenue spread across channels that bring customers who stay supports the high end of a range.

To show that, you need revenue per acquisition channel, with each subscription payment joined to the visit that brought the customer. See MRR by acquisition channel for how to get it, and the NRR calculator to measure how much revenue your existing customers keep.

SaaS valuation calculator FAQ

How do you value a SaaS company with an ARR multiple?

Multiply annual recurring revenue by a revenue multiple. Using a low and a high multiple gives a range rather than one number, which is closer to how offers and negotiations work.

What multiple should I use?

Use multiples from real offers, advisors or comparable deals you trust. The calculator has no built-in multiples because they change with the market, the buyer and the business, and a generic table can mislead.

Why do growth and churn matter for valuation?

They change what a buyer expects ARR to be next year and how much of today's revenue will still be there. Faster growth and lower churn usually support a higher multiple; high churn means revenue has to be replaced just to stand still.

Should I enter MRR or ARR?

Either. Pick the one you track in the Revenue input menu. MRR is multiplied by 12 to get ARR, and switching converts the number you entered.

Is this a formal valuation?

No. It is arithmetic on the numbers you enter. A real sale price also depends on profit, deal structure, due diligence and the buyer.

Which ARR do I enter if I only know my run rate?

The recurring run rate, with one-time sales left out and yearly plans spread over 12 months. In the made-up example on this page, a $30,000 month looks like $360,000 a year, but its ARR is $180,000, so every figure in the range is halved.

Is doubling ARR the same as doubling the multiple?

In the formula, yes: $600,000 at 6 times ARR and $1,200,000 at 3 times ARR both give $3,600,000. ARR comes from your customers and can be checked; the multiple is an assumption until someone makes an offer.
