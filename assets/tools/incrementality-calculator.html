The incrementality formulas

The control group shows what happens without the spend. Apply its conversion rate to the test group and you get the customers you would have expected anyway. Whatever is above that is incremental:

conversion rate = customers / visitors
lift = (test rate - control rate) / control rate
incremental customers = (test rate - control rate) × test visitors
incremental revenue = incremental customers × average revenue per customer
cost per incremental customer = spend / incremental customers
incremental ROAS = incremental revenue / spend

To see whether the difference is larger than chance, the calculator measures it against how much two groups of this size would differ if the spend changed nothing:

pooled rate = all customers / all visitors
standard error = √(pooled rate × (1 - pooled rate) × (1 / test visitors + 1 / control visitors))
z = (test rate - control rate) / standard error

A z above 1.96 or below -1.96 means a two-sided p-value under 0.05, which is larger than chance at 95 percent. The test needs a few customers to work with: if either group would have fewer than 5 customers, or fewer than 5 visitors who did not buy, at the pooled rate, the calculator says too few instead of giving a verdict.

A worked example

These are made-up numbers, the defaults above. An ad was shown to a test group of 40,000 visitors and held back from a control group of 10,000. The test group had 960 customers, a 2.40% conversion rate. Control had 200, or 2.00%.

  • Lift: (2.40% - 2.00%) / 2.00% = 20%.
  • Expected customers in test: 40,000 × 2.00% = 800.
  • Incremental customers: 960 - 800 = 160.
  • Incremental revenue: 160 × $50 = $8,000.
  • Cost per incremental customer: $4,000 / 160 = $25.
  • Incremental ROAS: $8,000 / $4,000 = 2x.

The pooled rate is 1,160 / 50,000 = 2.32%. The standard error is √(0.0232 × 0.9768 × (1 / 40,000 + 1 / 10,000)) = 0.001683, so z = 0.004 / 0.001683 = 2.38 and the p-value is 0.0175. The difference is larger than chance at 95 percent.

Counting all 960 customers as caused by the ad would give $48,000 / $4,000 = 12x. Most of those sales also appeared, in proportion, in the group that never got the ad. At a 60% gross margin, the $8,000 leaves $4,800 of gross profit, or $800 after the spend. The same 2x would lose money below a 50% margin, because the break-even ROAS is 1 divided by the gross margin. The ROAS calculator works that out for yours.

A "no" does not mean the spend did nothing. It means a test this small cannot tell the difference from luck, and the incremental customers it shows could easily be zero. Decide the size before you start and read the result once, at the end. Stopping on the first good day finds lifts that are not there.

How to set up the two groups

  • Split at random. People who chose to click are not comparable with people who did not. Use a holdout from your ad platform if it offers one, or hold a random part of your list back from an email.
  • Count everyone assigned. Include the people in the test group who never saw the ad. If you drop them from test but not from control, the groups no longer match. For an ad or email audience, enter people in place of visitors in both groups.
  • Change one thing. Keep prices, offers and other campaigns the same for both groups.
  • Use the same dates and counting rule. Count customers in both groups from your payments, over the same days, and leave enough time for late buyers to pay.
  • Count each person once. A customer who orders twice is one customer. Put the extra order in the average revenue per customer.

When the calculator shows a message instead of a number

  • Zero spend: lift and incremental customers still work, but there is nothing to divide for cost per incremental customer or incremental ROAS.
  • Zero customers: with none in either group there is nothing to compare. With none in control, lift has no baseline, though incremental customers can still be counted.
  • More customers than visitors: one of the two numbers is wrong, so nothing is calculated.
  • Negative lift: the test group bought less often. Incremental customers and revenue go below zero, and cost per incremental customer and incremental ROAS are not shown, because the spend gained nothing to price.

Incrementality and attribution answer different questions

Attribution credits each payment to a visit. It shows which channels your buyers came through, every day, without stopping any campaign. It does not say which of those buyers would have paid anyway. Splitting the credit over more visits does not change that: see multi-touch attribution for a small business. The gap tends to matter most for ads shown to people who already know you, which is why retargeting looks better under last touch than in a pause test.

A pause test compares two periods rather than two random groups, so season and other campaigns get in, and this calculator's chance check does not cover that. Virling does not split audiences or run holdouts. It records the visits and payments, so you can read revenue by campaign and the total for the dates of your test. Incrementality calculator FAQ

How do you calculate incrementality?

Subtract the control group's conversion rate from the test group's, then multiply by the visitors in the test group. With 2.4% in a test group of 40,000 and 2.0% in control, 0.4% of 40,000 is 160 incremental customers: the ones you would not expect without the spend.

How is incremental lift calculated?

Divide the difference between the two conversion rates by the control rate. A test group at 2.4% against a control group at 2.0% is a lift of 0.4 / 2.0 = 20%. Lift has no value when control has no customers, because there is no baseline to divide by.

What is incremental ROAS?

Incremental revenue divided by the spend being tested. It leaves out the sales that control suggests would have happened anyway, so with a positive baseline it is lower than a ROAS that counts every sale in the test group.

What does a negative lift mean?

The test group bought less often than control. If the difference is not larger than chance, the test does not show that the spend hurt sales, only that it found no gain. If it is larger than chance, first check that the groups were split at random and counted the same way.

Do the test and control groups have to be the same size?

No. Conversion rates are compared, and the control rate is scaled to the size of the test group. A very small control group makes the result less certain, though, so the same difference is harder to tell from chance.

Is an incrementality test the same as attribution?

No. Attribution credits recorded payments to visits by a rule. An incrementality test compares people who got the spend with people who did not, to estimate how many sales it caused. Attribution runs all the time on every channel; a test answers one question about one spend.
