The NRR formula

Net revenue retention compares what a fixed group of customers pays at the end of a period with what they paid at the start:

NRR = (starting MRR + expansion - contraction - churned MRR) / starting MRR

Gross revenue retention is the same without expansion, so it only counts losses and tops out at 100%:

GRR = (starting MRR - contraction - churned MRR) / starting MRR

Expansion is extra MRR from upgrades, added seats or usage. Contraction is MRR lost to downgrades from customers who stayed. Churned MRR is what cancelled customers paid. Leave out customers who signed up during the period: their MRR is new business, not retention.

A worked example

With the defaults above, your existing customers pay $20,000 MRR at the start of the period. Upgrades add $1,500, downgrades remove $400 and cancellations remove $600.

NRR is ($20,000 + $1,500 - $400 - $600) / $20,000 = $20,500 / $20,000 = 102.5%. The same customers pay more at the end than at the start, without counting any new sales.

GRR is ($20,000 - $400 - $600) / $20,000 = $19,000 / $20,000 = 95%. You kept 95% of the revenue you started with, and expansion more than covered the rest.

Edge cases in NRR

Compare each customer's MRR at the start of the period with their MRR at the end, and count the difference once. Use the same treatment every period:

CaseWhat to do
UpgradesExpansion, for the difference only: a move from $50 to $80 adds $30. Count it once the new price is billed.
DowngradesContraction, for the difference only. A customer who went from $50 to $80 and then to $40 within the period is $10 of contraction, not $30 of expansion and $40 of contraction.
Cancelled, still in the paid periodNo change until the paid period ends. Then the whole amount is churned MRR.
PausesChurned MRR if billing is stopped at the end of the period. A pause that starts and ends within the period is no loss. A customer already paused at the start is not in starting MRR, so leave them out when they resume.
Annual plansCount the yearly price divided by 12. These customers can rarely leave before their year ends, so they make a monthly NRR look better than it is: work it out apart from monthly plans, or measure NRR over a year.
Monthly to annualCompare the monthly amounts. A move from $50 a month to $480 a year is $40 of MRR, so $10 of contraction, even though more cash came in that day.
Win-backsA customer who left before the period started is not in starting MRR. Their return is neither expansion nor retention: leave it out, like a new customer. One who left and came back within the period counts at what they pay at the end.
Price increases and discounts that endExpansion, because the same customer pays more. Note how much of it there was, since it is not a customer choosing to buy more.
Usage chargesPut them in expansion only if your starting MRR includes usage too, measured the same way. Otherwise count a higher fixed commitment and leave the rest out.

NRR calculator FAQ

How is net revenue retention calculated?

Take the MRR your existing customers paid at the start of a period, add expansion, subtract contraction and churned MRR, and divide by the starting MRR. Revenue from customers who signed up during the period is left out, so new sales do not hide losses.

What is the difference between NRR and GRR?

Net revenue retention counts expansion from upgrades, extra seats and usage. Gross revenue retention leaves expansion out and only counts what was lost, so it can never be above 100%. NRR shows whether existing customers grow on their own; GRR shows how well you keep the revenue you already have.

What is a good NRR?

Above 100% means your existing customers pay more at the end of the period than at the start, even with no new sales. Below 100% means you need new customers to stand still. What is realistic depends on pricing: seat or usage based plans can expand, while a single flat plan mostly cannot. Compare against your own past periods first.

Should I calculate NRR monthly or annually?

Either works if you are consistent. Annual NRR is usually measured on the customers you had twelve months ago, comparing what they paid then with what they pay now. Monthly NRR moves more from one month to the next, especially with few customers.

Why does NRR differ by channel?

Customers from different channels arrive with different intent and budgets. A launch or a discount listing can bring customers who downgrade or leave, while search or referrals can bring customers who upgrade. Splitting retention by the channel that brought each customer shows which channels grow over time.
