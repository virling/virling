The GRR formula

Gross revenue retention is the share of starting recurring revenue that a fixed group of customers still pays at the end of a period, counting only losses:

GRR = (starting MRR - churned MRR - contraction MRR) / starting MRR

Starting MRR is what your existing customers paid at the start of the period. Churned MRR is what customers who cancelled used to pay. Contraction MRR is what customers who stayed but moved to a cheaper plan, fewer seats or less usage stopped paying. Customers who signed up during the period are left out, and so is expansion from those who upgraded.

A worked example

With the defaults above, your existing customers paid $20,000 MRR at the start of the month. Customers who cancelled took $600 with them, and customers who downgraded paid $400 less.

GRR is ($20,000 - $600 - $400) / $20,000 = $19,000 / $20,000 = 95%. You kept 95% of the recurring revenue you started with. If the same customers also upgraded by $1,500, NRR would be 102.5%, but GRR stays at 95%, because expansion does not count.

Edge cases in GRR

For each customer, count what they pay at the end of the period, but never more than they paid at the start. Whatever is missing is a loss. That rule settles most cases:

CaseWhat to do
UpgradesLeave them out. One customer's upgrade does not make up for another customer's downgrade, and a price increase does not lift GRR.
DowngradesContraction, for the part below what they paid at the start. From $50 to $80 and then to $40 within the period is $10. From $50 to $80 and back to $50 is nothing.
Cancelled, still in the paid periodNo loss until the paid period ends. Then the whole starting amount is churned MRR.
Failed cardsNo loss while your provider still retries the charge. If the subscription ends, it is churned MRR like any other, but keep the amount apart, because the fix is different. The failed payment recovery calculator shows what it is worth.
PausesChurned MRR if billing is stopped at the end of the period. A pause that starts and ends within the period is no loss. A customer already paused at the start is in neither number.
Annual plansCount the yearly price divided by 12, and keep them apart from monthly plans. For a fair figure, enter as starting MRR only the plans whose year ends in the period, then what those customers dropped.
Monthly to annualContraction if the yearly price per month is lower: $50 a month to $480 a year is $40 of MRR, so $10 lost. Note it apart from downgrades, since the customer committed for longer.
Win-backsA customer who left before the period started is not in starting MRR, so their return does not count. One who left and came back within the period counts at what they pay at the end, up to their starting amount. A return in a later period does not undo the loss.
DiscountsUse the price after the discount. A discount you give to keep a customer is contraction for as long as it runs.

What this number misses

GRR is one blended number for all your customers, and retention is rarely the same across the channels they came from. A launch, a deal listing or a discount code can bring customers who try a month and leave, while search, referrals or a niche community can bring customers who stay for years. A GRR of 95% can be an average of channels that keep nearly everything and channels that lose a large share every month, and the blended number will not tell you which is which. To see it, credit each customer's recurring revenue to the visit that brought them, then compare churned and contraction MRR per channel. GRR calculator FAQ

How is gross revenue retention calculated?

Take the MRR your existing customers paid at the start of a period, subtract the MRR lost to downgrades (contraction) and to cancellations (churned MRR), and divide by the starting MRR. Revenue from customers who signed up during the period and revenue from upgrades are both left out.

What is the difference between GRR and NRR?

Net revenue retention adds expansion from upgrades, extra seats and usage. Gross revenue retention leaves expansion out and only counts what was lost, so it can never be above 100%. GRR shows how well you keep the revenue you already have; NRR shows whether existing customers grow on their own.

Why can GRR not go above 100%?

GRR only subtracts losses from the starting MRR and never adds anything back. The best possible result is 100%, which means no existing customer downgraded or cancelled during the period.

Should I calculate GRR monthly or annually?

Either works if you are consistent. Annual GRR is usually measured on the customers you had twelve months ago, comparing what they paid then with what they pay now, minus any upgrades. Monthly GRR moves more from one month to the next, especially with few customers.

Why does GRR differ by channel?

Customers from different channels arrive with different intent and budgets. A launch or a discount listing can bring customers who downgrade or leave, while search or referrals can bring customers who stay. Splitting retention by the channel that brought each customer shows which channels bring revenue that lasts.
