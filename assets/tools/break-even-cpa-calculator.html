The break-even CPA formula

Cost per acquisition (CPA) is what you pay in marketing to win one paying customer. You break even when that cost equals the gross profit the customer brings. For one-time orders:

break-even CPA = average order value × gross margin × orders per customer

For a subscription, use the monthly price and the number of months a customer stays:

break-even CPA = monthly price × gross margin × months retained

Gross margin is the share of each sale left after the cost of the product, hosting, payment fees and delivery. To keep a profit after paying for the customer, take your target profit margin off the gross margin first:

target CPA = revenue per customer × (gross margin - target profit margin)

Here the target profit margin is the share of the customer's revenue you want left over after product costs and the cost of acquiring them. If it is as high as your gross margin, no CPA reaches it.

A worked example

This is an example with made-up numbers, the defaults above, not data from a real store. The store's average order is $80, its gross margin is 50% and a customer places 2.5 orders on average. Each customer brings $80 × 2.5 = $200 of revenue and $200 × 0.50 = $100 of gross profit, so the break-even CPA is $100. To keep a 20% profit margin, the store can pay up to $200 × (0.50 - 0.20) = $60 per customer. Switch to a monthly subscription at $49 with an 80% margin and 12 months retained, and the break-even CPA is $49 × 0.80 × 12 = $470.40.

In a month the store spends $20,000 on marketing and wins 500 new customers, so its blended CPA is $20,000 / 500 = $40, well under both limits. It takes in $100,000 that month from 1,250 orders: 500 first orders and 750 from customers won earlier. The MER calculator uses the same store, where that is an MER of 5x against a break-even of 2x.

How a good average CPA can hide a losing channel

Customers from Organic search, Email and Direct pull the average down, so more spend on Paid could keep the blended number under $100 while every extra customer costs more than they bring.

The limit can differ by channel too. If Paid customers placed 3.5 orders instead of 2.5, their break-even CPA would be $80 × 0.50 × 3.5 = $140 and $120 would pay back. Compare each channel with the orders its own customers place, over a range long enough for them to come back.

Break-even CPA needs CPA per channel

The limit is the easy half. The hard half is knowing what each channel really costs per customer, and how many orders or months the customers from that channel actually last. Ad platforms count the conversions they can see within their own attribution windows, so the same customer can be claimed twice. Break-even CPA calculator FAQ

How do you calculate break-even CPA?

Multiply the revenue a customer brings over their lifetime by your gross margin. For one-time orders that is average order value times orders per customer; for a subscription it is monthly price times months retained. An $80 order at a 50% margin with 2.5 orders per customer gives a break-even CPA of $100.

What is the difference between break-even CPA and target CPA?

Break-even CPA is the most you can pay for a customer and still make zero profit. Target CPA is lower: it leaves the profit margin you choose. With $200 of revenue per customer, a 50% gross margin and a 20% target profit margin, the target CPA is $60.

Should I use first-order value or lifetime value?

Use first-order value, by setting orders per customer to 1, if you need each customer to pay back on the first purchase. Use expected repeat orders or months retained if you can wait for the profit to come in, and check that your actual repeat rate supports the number you enter.

How does break-even CPA relate to break-even ROAS?

Both mark the point where gross profit covers ad spend. Break-even ROAS is 1 divided by your gross margin and is measured per sale; break-even CPA is measured per customer and includes repeat orders, so it can allow a higher cost when customers come back.

What costs does this calculator leave out?

It counts only the gross profit per customer against the cost to acquire them. Salaries, tools, agency fees, refunds and chargebacks are not included, so keep some room below the break-even CPA for them.

Can a channel lose money when my average CPA is below break-even?

Yes. The average includes customers who cost little to win, such as those from email or direct visits. In the made-up example on this page the blended CPA is $40 against a break-even of $100, while one channel pays $120 per customer and loses $20 on each.

Should every channel have the same break-even CPA?

Only if its customers spend the same. Work out the limit with each channel's own order value, margin and orders per customer. At an $80 order and a 50% margin, 2.5 orders allow $100 and 3.5 orders allow $140.
