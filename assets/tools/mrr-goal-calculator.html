The formulas in plain words

First work out the average MRR one customer brings. A yearly plan counts as one twelfth of its price each month:

MRR per customer = monthly price × (1 − yearly share) + yearly price / 12 × yearly share

Then divide the goal by it, rounding up because you cannot have part of a customer:

paying customers needed = target MRR / MRR per customer

Every month some of those customers cancel. To stay at the goal you have to replace them:

new customers per month = paying customers × monthly churn

And to win those new customers, enough people have to visit:

visitors per month = new customers per month / visitor-to-paid conversion

The new customers and visitors are what it takes to hold MRR at the goal once you reach it; getting there from where you are now needs more on top. The calculator applies one churn rate to every customer. Yearly customers can only cancel at renewal, so if many pay yearly, use the churn rate you measure across all customers.

A worked example

These are round illustrative numbers, the same as the defaults above, not data from a real business. A product wants $10,000 of MRR. Its monthly plan costs $50 and its yearly plan $480, and a quarter of customers pick yearly. Each yearly customer adds $480 / 12 = $40 a month, so the average customer brings $50 × 0.75 + $40 × 0.25 = $47.50 of MRR.

$10,000 / $47.50 = 210.5, so the product needs 211 paying customers. With 4% monthly churn it loses 211 × 4% = 8.44 customers a month, so it needs 9 new customers every month to hold $10,000. If 2% of visitors become paying customers, that takes 9 / 2% = 450 visitors a month.

Which visitors convert matters more than how many

The visitors-needed number assumes every visitor converts at the same rate. They do not: visitors from a search for your product name or a recommendation in a newsletter often convert far better than visitors from a broad social post, so the same 450 visitors can bring very different numbers of customers depending on where they come from. Measure conversion for each channel with the conversion rate calculator, check your churn figure with the churn rate calculator, and read how to see MRR by acquisition channel to find the channels whose customers pay and stay.

Split the goal by channel with revenue per visitor

The 9 new customers a month in the worked example bring 9 × $47.50 = $427.50 of new MRR from 450 visitors, so each visitor brings $0.95. That is revenue per visitor, counted in new MRR instead of payments, and it is what lets you split the goal. Here is the same month divided over three made-up channels, at $47.50 a customer:

Another $95 of new MRR takes 250 more visitors from social, 75 from organic search or 34 from the newsletter. Channels with few customers move a lot when one person signs up, so use several months before you set targets.

For your own figures, the Channels card in Virling shows visitors, revenue and revenue per visitor for each channel, net of refunds. That revenue is money collected: renewals count for the channel of the first payment, and a yearly plan counts in full in the month it is paid. For new MRR per visitor, multiply the channel's new customers by the average MRR per customer and divide by its visitors.

Three mistakes with an MRR goal

  1. Treating the number that holds the goal as the number that reaches it. Going from 100 customers to 211 in 12 months takes 111 more, or 9.25 a month, on top of replacing cancellations. At 4% churn that is about 13 new customers a month at the start and about 18 near the end, not 9. The month-by-month MRR forecast shows the path.
  2. Counting a yearly payment as one month of MRR. Ten yearly sales at $480 put $4,800 in the bank but add $400 of MRR. Count the full payment and you think the goal is met long before it is.
  3. Using the list price when customers pay less. If a coupon takes 20% off for everyone, a $50 plan collects $40, and $10,000 takes 250 customers instead of 200. Enter the price people actually pay, before tax.

MRR goal calculator FAQ

How many customers do I need to reach my MRR goal?

Divide the target MRR by the average monthly revenue per customer and round up. At $47.50 a customer, $10,000 of MRR takes 10,000 / 47.50 = 210.5, so 211 paying customers.

How do yearly plans change the number?

A yearly plan counts as one twelfth of its price each month. If yearly plans are cheaper per month than monthly ones, a larger yearly share lowers the average MRR per customer, so you need more customers for the same goal.

Why do I need new customers just to stay at my goal?

Some customers cancel every month. At 4% monthly churn, 211 customers lose about 8.4 a month, so you need 9 new customers each month only to hold MRR level. Growth needs more on top.

What conversion rate should I use?

Your own visitor-to-paid rate: new paying customers divided by unique visitors over the same period. It varies widely between products and between channels, so a measured number beats a general average.

Does the calculator store my numbers?

No. The calculation runs in your browser and nothing is uploaded or saved.

How does churn change the new customers I need?

In proportion. Doubling monthly churn doubles the customers you have to replace: for a $10,000 goal at $50 a month, 4% churn takes 8 new customers a month and 8% takes 16. The number of paying customers at the goal stays 200.

Can I use revenue per visitor to plan an MRR goal?

Yes, if you count new MRR instead of all payments. Divide the new MRR you need each month by the new MRR one visitor brings: $427.50 at $0.95 a visitor is 450 visitors. Revenue per visitor from payments also includes renewals and full yearly payments, so it would make the visitors needed look too low.
