The POAS formula

Profit on ad spend is the gross profit your ads brought divided by what you spent on them:

POAS = gross profit from ads / ad spend

Gross profit here is what is left of the revenue after the costs that come with each sale:

gross profit = revenue - refunds - product cost - payment fees - shipping

A POAS of 1 means the ads paid for themselves and left nothing over. Above 1 they earned more gross profit than they cost; below 1 every dollar of ad spend brought back less than a dollar of profit. Because the costs are already inside the number, the break-even point for POAS is always 1, whatever you sell.

POAS and ROAS side by side

Return on ad spend divides revenue, not profit, by ad spend:

ROAS = revenue from ads / ad spend

ROAS is what ad platforms report, because revenue is what they can see. It hides the cost of each sale, so a ROAS of 3x can be a good month for a store with high margins and a loss for one that resells products with thin margins. POAS answers the question you actually have: did the ads make money? The calculator shows both so you can compare its answer with what your ad account says.

To turn POAS back into a ROAS target, the calculator works out the break-even ROAS for your costs:

break-even ROAS = revenue / gross profit

This is the ROAS at which gross profit exactly equals ad spend, the same point as a POAS of 1. It assumes your costs grow in step with revenue, which holds for product cost, fees and shipping but not for fixed costs. Use it as the floor to set in your ad platform, not as a goal. If you only know your gross margin, the ROAS calculator gives the same floor as 1 / gross margin.

What goes into each input

  • Ad spend: what you paid the platform for the period or campaign, including any platform fees or taxes you were charged.
  • Revenue from those ads: the sales tied to the ads, before refunds and without sales tax.
  • Product cost or gross margin: either the dollar cost of the goods in the orders you kept, or the share of revenue left after product cost. If returned goods go back on the shelf, leave them out of the dollar cost.
  • Payment fees: the processor's cut as a share of revenue. Processors often keep the fee on a refunded charge, so it is taken on all revenue.
  • Shipping you paid: postage and packing you covered, not the shipping the customer paid you.
  • Refunds: the money you gave back on those orders.

A worked example

Fernwood Candles is a made-up store, and its numbers are the defaults above. In one month it spent $2,000 on ads that brought $8,000 of revenue. It refunded $300, the candles in the orders it kept cost $3,000 to make, its payment processor took 3% of $8,000, which is $240, and it paid $400 for shipping.

Gross profit is $8,000 - $300 - $3,000 - $240 - $400 = $4,060. POAS is $4,060 / $2,000 = 2.03, so each dollar of ad spend brought back $2.03 of gross profit, and the ads made $4,060 - $2,000 = $2,060. ROAS is $8,000 / $2,000 = 4x. The break-even ROAS is $8,000 / $4,060 = 1.97x: below that, the same mix of costs would turn the ads into a loss.

Now say the store had spent $4,500 to get the same $8,000. ROAS would be $8,000 / $4,500 = 1.78x, which an ad account would show as nearly twice the money back. POAS would be $4,060 / $4,500 = 0.90, and the ads would have lost $4,500 - $4,060 = $440. ROAS alone would not have shown that loss.

When the calculator gives a message instead of a number

Some inputs have no sensible answer, and a number would mislead you, so the calculator says so in words:

  • Zero ad spend: POAS and ROAS divide by spend, so with no spend there is nothing to divide. Gross profit is still shown.
  • A gross margin over 100%: that would mean the product costs less than nothing. Check whether you entered a markup instead of a margin.
  • Refunds larger than revenue: the refunds probably include orders from outside this period or campaign. Use the refunds on the orders the ads brought.
  • A loss: the calculator shows the amount and says it is a loss, and if the orders lose money even before ad spend, it says that no ROAS can break even.

Which number to change first

  • POAS below 1 on one campaign: move or cut its spend before anything else.
  • POAS below 1 everywhere: look at price, product cost and shipping, because no ad change fixes a margin problem.
  • Gross profit below zero before ads: the orders themselves lose money; stop paying to get more of them.
  • High refunds: find out which products or campaigns they come from, since the ad spend that won them is gone.
  • POAS well above 1: raise spend in steps and check the POAS of the added spend, which is usually lower than the average.

POAS needs profit matched to the channel

The division is easy; the hard part is knowing which revenue and which refunds belong to which ads. Ad platforms count the conversions they can see within their own attribution windows, and a sale that touched two platforms can be counted by both, so adding up platform revenue overstates what you made. To get a POAS you can trust per channel, each payment and each refund has to be tied to the visit that started it. POAS calculator FAQ

What is POAS?

POAS, or profit on ad spend, is the gross profit your ads brought divided by what you spent on them. Gross profit here is revenue after refunds, product cost, payment fees and shipping. A POAS of 1 means the ads paid for themselves and nothing more.

How is POAS different from ROAS?

ROAS divides revenue by ad spend, so it ignores what each sale costs you. POAS divides gross profit by ad spend, so the costs are already inside it. Two campaigns with the same ROAS can have very different POAS if one sells products with lower margins.

What is a good POAS?

Above 1 the ads earn more gross profit than they cost, and below 1 they lose money. How far above 1 you need to be depends on the costs this calculator leaves out, such as salaries, software and agency fees, so there is no single good number.

How is break-even ROAS worked out here?

It is the ROAS at which gross profit exactly covers ad spend: revenue divided by gross profit. It assumes your costs grow in step with revenue. If your orders lose money before any ad spend, there is no ROAS at which the ads break even.

Should refunds count in POAS?

Yes. A refunded order brings no profit but the ad spend that won it is still gone, and payment processors often keep the fee on a refunded charge. Leaving refunds out makes POAS look better than the money you kept.
