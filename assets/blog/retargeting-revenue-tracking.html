Retargeting ROI tracking: why last touch flatters it

By Devendra Variya, co-founder of Virling. Published 2026-11-24.

Retargeting ROI tracking takes two numbers. The first is the revenue credited to your retargeting campaigns, divided by what they cost. It is easy to get, and last touch makes it look high, because retargeting is shown to people who already visited and were more likely to buy. The second is what those ads added. Tag retargeting apart from prospecting, compare first and last touch for the same payments, then pause retargeting for a few weeks and count all your sales. The sales that remain are the ones that came without it.

Why retargeting always looks good under last touch

  • It picks people who are already interested. A retargeting audience is made of people who read your pricing page or filled a cart. Some would have come back and paid with no ad.
  • It arrives late. The ad can only run after a first visit, so its click tends to be one of the last before the payment, and last touch credits the latest visit.
  • The other channels did the earlier work. Search, a post or a prospecting ad brought the buyer. Under last touch they get nothing for that sale.
  • Ad platforms can count views too. If the platform counts a purchase after an ad was only seen, the same buyer can be claimed with no click at all.

None of this means retargeting is worthless. It means credited revenue cannot tell a sale it caused from one it only touched.

How first touch and last touch differ

Virling credits each payment to one visit, picked by the site’s attribution model:

  • Last non-direct, the default, credits the latest visit in the window that was not Direct.
  • First touch credits the earliest visit in the window.
  • Last touch credits the latest visit in the window, Direct included.

The third row shows that last non-direct can favour retargeting more than last touch does. The fourth shows the opposite: an ad that was only seen gets no credit under any model that works from visits. And first touch is not the fair answer either, because an ad shown only to past visitors is almost never the first visit. One rule gives retargeting nearly everything, the other nearly nothing, and neither says what would have happened without it. For choosing a rule, see first-touch or last-touch attribution, and try your own journeys in the attribution model calculator.

The window matters too. First touch means the earliest visit inside it, so with a short window the visit that introduced the buyer can fall outside and the retargeting click becomes the first one left. See attribution window. Virling uses 90 days by default and offers 30, 60, 90 or 365.

Tag retargeting separately from prospecting

If both kinds of ad share a campaign name, their revenue is one row and you cannot judge either. Give each its own utm_campaign, and start the name with what it is:

https://yoursite.com/?utm_source=facebook&utm_medium=paid_social&utm_campaign=prospecting-spring
https://yoursite.com/?utm_source=facebook&utm_medium=paid_social&utm_campaign=retargeting-cart-30d
https://yoursite.com/?utm_source=google&utm_medium=cpc&utm_campaign=retargeting-pricing-page
  • Keep a paid medium. cpc, ppc, paid, paidsearch, paid_social and paidsocial are the Paid channel in Virling. utm_medium=retargeting, display and cpm are not, so those clicks land in the channel of their referrer or source. See how channels are decided. Native ads follow the same rule; see native ads revenue tracking.
  • One campaign per audience. People who left a cart and people who read one article behave differently, so name the audience and its length in days.
  • Exclude past visitors from prospecting. Otherwise a prospecting campaign does some retargeting and takes credit for it. Exclude recent buyers from retargeting.
  • Use one spelling. A campaign tagged two ways becomes two rows. The UTM builder makes the links.

The tag also marks the visit. In Virling, a page that loads with UTM tags different from the visit’s own starts a new visit, so a tagged retargeting click gets its own line in the buyer’s journey even when they were on your site minutes earlier. See how attribution works.

Read the credited revenue

  1. Add the script and link checkout. Put the Virling script on every page the ads land on, connect your payment provider, and pass the visitor ID into checkout.
  2. Open the Campaign tab in the Channels card. Each utm_campaign is a row with visitors, revenue and revenue per visitor, after refunds.
  3. Work out the return. Put the campaign’s revenue and the cost you read in the ad platform into the ROAS calculator. This is the credited return.
  4. Look at the journeys. Open a payment to see Credited to, First touch, Time to pay and each visit before it, with its campaign. Note which channel came first on the retargeted sales.
  5. Compare the models. Switch the site to first touch in Site settings, note the same campaigns, and switch back. It rebuilds every payment, history included, and nothing is lost by switching.

A campaign with high revenue under last non-direct and very little under first touch is one that mostly follows other channels. That is normal for retargeting, and it is the reason to run the test below.

A simple pause test

Pausing shows what sales remain when the ads are gone:

  1. Pick the length. Run it longer than most buyers take to pay. The Time to pay card shows the median per channel.
  2. Record a baseline. Over a period of the same length with retargeting on, note total payments and revenue for the whole site, and the spend.
  3. Pause retargeting only. Leave prospecting, prices, emails and offers as they are, and avoid holidays, sales and launches.
  4. Skip the first days. Someone who clicked just before the pause may still pay. Leave the same number of days out of the baseline.
  5. Compare totals. Look at all payments, not the retargeting row, which will fall whatever happens. If the total holds, the same buyers came back another way.
  6. Turn it back on and look again. If the total rises when the ads return, the difference is more likely to be real.

A worked example

Example only: made-up numbers for an imaginary store, not results from a real business. Every order is $50, the gross margin is 60%, and both periods are four weeks with the same prospecting spend.

Four weeksRetargeting onRetargeting paused
Retargeting spend$900$0
Payments credited to retargeting900
All payments300276
All revenue$15,000$13,800
  • Credited return: 90 payments are $4,500, and $4,500 / $900 is a ROAS of 5x.
  • What remained: the total fell by 24 payments, not 90. The other 66 sales still happened without the ads, if nothing else changed.
  • Added return: 24 payments are $1,200, and $1,200 / $900 is 1.33x. At a 60% margin the break-even ROAS is 1 / 0.60 = 1.67x.
  • Added profit: $1,200 of revenue leaves $720 of gross profit, so after $900 of spend the ads lost $180 in those four weeks.

Be careful with a gap this size. With about 300 payments in a period, two ordinary periods can differ by a couple of dozen by chance alone, so 24 is a reason to repeat the test, not proof. A pause test also compares two different times, so season, news and other campaigns get in. Where your ad platform offers a holdout, use it: Google describes Conversion Lift as a controlled experiment that separates your audience into two groups, though it is not available to every account (source: Google Ads Help).

The limits of cookie-based audiences

Retargeting by cookie reaches only the past visitors an ad platform can recognise, and this affects the results you measure:

  • Some browsers block the cookie. Safari has blocked third-party cookies by default since 2020 (source: WebKit). In April 2025 Google said Chrome would keep its approach of letting people choose (source: Privacy Sandbox). Audiences built this way cover some browsers better than others.
  • Consent and blockers remove people. Visitors who decline marketing cookies or block the tag should not be in the audience, so it is not a sample of all your visitors.
  • A cookie is a browser, not a person. Someone who read on a phone and pays on a laptop can stay in the audience after buying. See cross-device attribution.
  • Small lists do not run. Google Ads asks for at least 100 active visitors or users in the last 30 days, and keeps people in a segment for 30 days unless you change it, up to 540 (source: Google Ads Help).
  • Your own cookie has limits too. Virling does not use third-party cookies. Its first-party virling_vid cookie is set by the script, and Safari caps cookies set that way at seven days (source: WebKit). A buyer who returns after a longer gap can look new, and the retargeting click then looks like the first visit.

The last point means first touch can credit retargeting with more than it earned. A pause test does not depend on cookies in this way, because it counts every payment.

What to do with the answer

  • Sales hold when paused. Cut the spend, shorten the audience, or keep only the part that needs a reminder, such as abandoned carts.
  • Sales fall by more than the ads cost in profit. Keep it, and use the added return, not the credited one, when you compare it with other channels.
  • You cannot tell. Run it again for longer, and do not raise the budget on the credited number alone.

For the campaigns that bring people the first time, see Google Ads revenue tracking and Meta Ads revenue tracking.

Frequently asked questions

How do I track retargeting ROI?

Tag retargeting ads with their own utm_campaign, connect your payment provider, and divide the revenue credited to that campaign, after refunds, by what the ads cost. Treat that as the revenue retargeting touched. To estimate what it added, pause it for a few weeks and compare total payments with a similar period when it ran.

Why does retargeting look so good under last touch?

Because it is shown to people who already visited, and a click on it often comes late in the journey. Last touch credits the latest visit, so a retargeting click just before the payment takes the whole payment, even when another channel brought the buyer and they would have come back anyway.

Should I judge retargeting by first touch instead?

Not by itself. Retargeting reaches people who have already been on your site, so it is rarely the first recorded visit and first touch gives it almost nothing. Use first touch to see which channels introduce buyers, and a pause test to see what retargeting adds.

How should I tag retargeting campaigns?

Use a paid medium such as cpc or paid_social, the platform as utm_source, and a utm_campaign that starts with retargeting, such as retargeting-cart-30d. Avoid utm_medium=retargeting, which is not a paid medium in Virling. Keep new audiences in campaigns that start with prospecting.

How long should a retargeting pause test run?

Longer than the time most buyers take from their first visit to paying, and long enough to collect a useful number of payments. Keep prices, other ads and emails the same, avoid holidays and launches, and repeat the test if the difference is small.

Can Virling see people who saw a retargeting ad but did not click?

No. Virling records visits to your site, so an ad that was seen and not clicked leaves no visit and gets no credit. A pause test can still pick up its effect, because it compares total payments.
