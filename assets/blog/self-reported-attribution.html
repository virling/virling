Self-reported attribution vs tracked attribution

By Devendra Variya, co-founder of Virling. Published 2026-11-24.

Self-reported attribution means asking buyers how they heard about you and crediting the source they name. Tracked attribution records the visits before a payment instead. The two answer different questions: a survey answer is what the buyer remembers, and a tracked channel is a visit that was recorded. Use tracked channels to compare links and campaigns by revenue, use the answers to find sources that leave no click, such as podcasts, word of mouth and private chats, and read the two side by side.

What self-reported attribution is

It is one question, usually “How did you hear about us?”, asked at signup, after checkout or on a sales call. The answer is stored with the customer, and you count customers or revenue per answer. It needs no cookie and no tag, and the buyer does the attributing.

Tracked attribution works from the other end. It stores each visit with its referrer and UTM tags, carries a visitor ID into checkout, and credits the payment to one of the visits. What revenue attribution is explains that join step by step.

What it catches that clicks miss

  • Podcasts and talks. Someone hears your name and types the address or searches for it later. The visit is Direct or Organic search, and only the buyer knows which show it was.
  • Word of mouth. A recommendation said out loud has no link at all. Word of mouth attribution covers how to pair share links with the answer.
  • Private chats. A link pasted in a group chat or a direct message often arrives with no referrer, so it lands in Direct. That is dark social traffic.
  • A change of device or person. A buyer who read about you on a phone and paid on a work laptop looks like two visitors. A colleague who signs up for the team may never have clicked the link that started it.
  • Blocked visits. A visitor who blocks tracking scripts leaves no visit, but can still answer a question.

What it gets wrong

  • Memory. People forget the first time they saw you, and name the last or the most memorable one.
  • The last step. “Google” often means they searched your name after hearing it somewhere else.
  • The list itself. People pick an option near the top or the first that is close enough, and a source that is not on the list goes uncounted.
  • Missing answers. People who skip the question may not be like those who answer, so answers do not scale up cleanly to everyone.
  • No detail. “Social media” does not say which post, campaign or landing page, so you cannot use it to compare two ads.
  • One answer for a long path. A buyer who met you in three places still picks one. This matters most in B2B, where a purchase can take weeks and several people: see B2B SaaS attribution for small teams.

When to ask

  • SaaS: on the signup form or the first onboarding screen. Signup is closer to the moment they found you than the first payment is.
  • Stores: on the thank-you page, where the question cannot slow the purchase down. Ask first-time buyers only.
  • Sales-led products: on the demo form or the first call. Write the answer into your CRM in the buyer's own words.

Ask once, and make it optional. A required question fills up with rushed answers.

How to word the question

  1. Ask about the first time. “How did you first hear about us?” asks for the introduction. “What brought you here today?” asks for the last step, which tracking often sees already. Pick one and keep it, since answers to different questions cannot be compared.
  2. Decide between a list and a text box. With few customers, an open text box gives names you did not expect. With many, a list is easier to count.
  3. Keep a list short and single choice. About ten options, with examples in brackets, such as “Online community (Reddit, Slack, Discord)”. Shuffle the order if your form can.
  4. End with “Other (please specify)” and a text box. When one name keeps coming up there, give it its own option.
  5. Follow up on vague answers. For “Podcast” or “Friend or colleague”, an optional “Which one?” or “Who can we thank?” gets the detail.

The how did you hear about us template has answer lists for SaaS and stores that you can edit and copy.

How to compare answers with tracked channels

  1. Pick a finished period and list the new paying customers in it. Leave out renewals.
  2. Next to each customer, write their answer from your own records, and “No answer” when there is none.
  3. Add the tracked channel of their first payment. Match on the payment or customer ID from your payment provider, not on dates or amounts.
  4. Group by answer, and count which tracked channels sit behind it.

The result fits in two columns. These numbers are made up to show the layout, for 60 new customers.

  • Both agree. Trust the source, and use the tracked side for the campaign and page.
  • Tracked is Direct or Unattributed, and the buyer names a source. The answer fills a gap. Give that source a tagged link so the next visit can be seen.
  • Tracked is Organic search, and the buyer names a person or a show. They probably searched your name. Check whether the landing page was the homepage.
  • Tracked is a tagged link, and the buyer names something else. Both can be true: the answer may be where they first heard of you, and the click the visit that got credit. Keep both and do not count the revenue twice.

Do not add the two columns together, or move revenue from one to the other. Keep the tracked numbers as your ledger and the answers as a second reading. With a handful of customers per answer, look for the same pattern over several months before you move budget.

Frequently asked questions

What is self-reported attribution?

It is crediting a customer to the source they name when you ask how they heard about you. The answer comes from the buyer's memory, usually through one question on a signup form, a thank-you page or a sales call, rather than from a recorded click.

Is self-reported attribution accurate?

It is accurate about some things and weak on others. It can name a podcast, a friend or a private chat that left no click, but people forget, name the last thing they saw, pick the first option or skip the question. Treat it as evidence, not a count.

What is the difference between self-reported and tracked attribution?

Self-reported attribution records what the buyer remembers. Tracked attribution records visits, from referrers and UTM tags, and joins them to payments. One can name a source that left no click, the other can name the exact link, page and campaign.

When should I ask how a customer heard about me?

Once, close to the decision: at signup or the first onboarding screen for a SaaS, on the thank-you page for a store, and on the first call or demo form for sales-led deals. Ask new customers only.

Should the question be a list or a text box?

Both work. An open text box gives names and stories but has to be sorted by hand. A list is easier to count but leads the answer. A short single-choice list that ends with Other and a text box is a fair middle.
