Multi-touch attribution for a small business

By Devendra Variya, co-founder of Virling. Published 2026-11-19, updated 2026-11-25.

Multi-touch attribution splits the credit for one sale across several of the visits that came before it, instead of giving all of it to one visit. A customer who read a post, searched your name and clicked an email before paying gives each of those a share. Linear and position-based are the two common rules for the split. For a small SaaS or store with short buying cycles, one single-touch model plus a look at each customer's journey usually answers the same question.

Single-touch and multi-touch models

  • First touch: all credit to the first visit
  • Last touch: all credit to the last visit before the payment, Direct included
  • Last non-direct: all credit to the last visit that came from a real source, skipping typed URLs and bookmarks
  • Linear: equal credit to every visit
  • Position-based (40/20/40): 40% to the first visit, 40% to the last, and the other 20% split equally between the ones in the middle

The first three are single-touch. The last two are multi-touch. Virling offers the first three: it credits each payment to one visit and has no linear or position-based model, so those two columns below are for comparison.

A multi-touch attribution example

This is a made-up example, not a real customer. Someone finds you in a Reddit thread, comes back a few days later from a Google search, clicks a link in your newsletter, then types your URL and pays $120.

1. Reddit$30$48$120$0$0
2. Google search$30$12$0$0$0

Every column adds up to $120. Linear says all four mattered equally. Position-based says Reddit and the final visit mattered most. First touch says Reddit found the customer, last touch says they came back on their own, and last non-direct says the newsletter brought them back to buy. A store journey: three visits in a week

This one is made up too. Someone clicks an Instagram ad on day 1, finds your store through a Google search on day 4, and opens a link in your newsletter on day 7. They place a $90 order during that third visit.

VisitLinearPosition-basedFirst touchLast touchLast non-direct
1. Instagram ad, day 1$30$36$90$0$0
2. Google search, day 4$30$18$0$0$0
3. Newsletter, day 7$30$36$0$90$90

Every column adds up to $90. The last visit came from a real source, so last touch and last non-direct agree on the newsletter. Only first touch credits the ad. All three visits fall within a week, so even a 30-day window keeps them all, and one question is left: whether you want to know what introduced the buyer or what brought them back.

A SaaS journey: a trial and two renewals

Another made-up journey. Someone reads a LinkedIn post on day 1, searches for you on day 9 and clicks a newsletter link on day 15. On day 16 they type your URL and start a 14-day trial of a $60 a month plan. The card is charged on day 30 and the plan renews twice, so the customer pays $180 in three charges. The table shows all three together.

VisitLinearPosition-basedFirst touchLast touchLast non-direct
1. LinkedIn post, day 1$45$72$180$0$0
2. Google search, day 9$45$18$0$0$0
3. Newsletter, day 15$45$18$0$0$180

Every column adds up to $180. The renewals have no visits of their own, so each one takes the credit of the first charge: under linear every visit gets $15 of each $60, and position-based gives $24, $6, $6 and $24. A report that drops the renewals would show a third of these amounts. Virling copies the credited visit of the first payment to each renewal; see subscription renewal attribution. The trial also makes the cycle longer: the LinkedIn post is 29 days before the first charge, so a trial two days longer would push it out of a 30-day window counted back from that charge. When Virling has recorded the trial start and the charge carries no visitor ID, it counts back from the trial start instead, as in the renewals docs.

When a small team needs more than one model

  • A channel looks weak under last touch but you suspect it introduces people. Check first touch before you drop it
  • Customers often take weeks and several visits to buy, so the first and last visits come from different channels
  • You are deciding where to spend money, and want to see whether the answer changes when the credit moves

If the two single-touch views agree, the split matters little. If they disagree, you have learned that one channel starts journeys and another finishes them, which is the main thing a multi-touch model would tell you.

When the journey of each payment answers it

Small businesses have tens or hundreds of payments a month, not millions. At that size you can open the payments behind a channel and read the visits before each one. That shows the real order of channels, with no weighting rule in between. A linear or position-based split is an estimate built from those same journeys; reading them directly is often clearer. A buyer who visits more than once on different devices shows up as two journeys unless they identified themselves on both; cross-device attribution covers when the two can be joined.

Keep the attribution window in mind too. Visits older than the window earn no credit under any model.

Limits every model shares

A model only divides credit between the visits that were recorded and joined to the buyer. None of them, single-touch or multi-touch, can fix these gaps:

  • Untracked visits: a blocked script leaves no visit, and neither does a podcast mention or a recommendation made in person. A visit that was not recorded earns nothing. Its share goes to the visits that were, or to no channel when there are none; see ad blockers and missing revenue
  • Other devices: a visit on a phone and a payment on a laptop are two journeys unless the buyer identified themselves on both, as covered above
  • Lost referrers: links opened from chat apps and email often arrive with no source and count as Direct. Linear and position-based then give Direct a share that belongs elsewhere; see dark social traffic
  • Window length: a visit older than the window is dropped before the split. In the SaaS journey, a window too short for the LinkedIn post would make the Google search the first visit, under first touch and position-based alike
  • Sales that would have happened anyway: a model credits visits, it does not show cause. To check whether a channel adds sales, run one of the incrementality tests for a small budget and work out the lift with the incrementality calculator. Frequently asked questions

What is multi-touch attribution?

It splits the credit for one sale across several of the visits before it, instead of giving all of it to one visit.

What is the difference between linear and position-based attribution?

Linear gives every visit an equal share. Position-based gives 40% to the first visit, 40% to the last, and splits the other 20% between the visits in the middle.

Does a small business need multi-touch attribution?

Usually not at first. With short buying cycles, comparing first touch with last non-direct and reading the visits before each payment answers the same question.

Does Virling support multi-touch attribution?

Not yet. Virling offers last non-direct, first touch and last touch, and shows every visit before each payment. Linear and position-based models are not available yet.

Is multi-touch attribution more accurate than single-touch?

Not necessarily. The split is a rule, not a measurement. It spreads credit more evenly, but the weights are chosen in advance, not learned from your sales.
