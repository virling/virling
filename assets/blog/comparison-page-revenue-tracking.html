Do comparison pages bring paying customers? How to find out

By Devendra Variya, co-founder of Virling. Published 2026-11-21.

A comparison page brings paying customers when the buyers who land on it, or who read it last before checkout, actually pay. Ranking for “Product A vs Product B” proves the page gets found. Whether it sells shows up only once each payment is tied to the visit and the pages that came before it.

This guide is for SaaS founders with “X vs Y” and “alternatives” pages, written or planned, who want to know if they pay for the hours. It looks at the searches that bring people to these pages, at the two jobs a comparison page can do, and at how to read both in Virling. Every product name here is a placeholder, such as Product A and Product B.

The searches that reach comparison pages

  • “Product A alternatives”. A broad search from people who use a tool, or looked at one, and want out. Many have never heard of you, so this page usually has to introduce you as well as compare.
  • “Product A vs Product B”. A narrow search from someone down to two options. Fewer people type it, and those who do are close to a decision.
  • “Your product vs Product A”. Someone who already knows your name and wants a reason to pick you, or not. These readers often found you somewhere else first.
  • “Cheaper than Product A” or “Product A pricing”. Price is the trigger. A page that answers it plainly, with your own price on it, gives this reader what they came for.

What you need first

Your payment provider has to be connected to Virling, and checkout has to carry the visitor ID, or no page report can show revenue. The checkout guide shows how to pass it for each provider.

Landing page or page seen before paying

Each payment, after refunds, is credited to one earlier session, named by its first page: by default the latest one in a 90-day window that did not arrive as Direct, so a buyer who found your comparison page through search and later typed your address in still credits that page. The attribution model page explains the choices.

On the dashboard’s Pages card, the Landing page tab answers whether a comparison page brings buyers in, and the Before paying tab answers whether it is the last thing they read before they pay. A page can be strong on one tab and absent from the other, and that is not a failure in itself.

  • Alternatives pages are mostly landing pages. Their readers come from search and many have not met you yet, so judge them on the Landing page tab: rows under a shared path such as /alternatives/, sorted by revenue per visitor, with the green or red chip against your site average.
  • “You vs them” pages are mostly read before paying. Their readers already know you, so they often land on the homepage or a post and open the comparison when they are about to decide. Judge them on Before paying. If one shows up there often, link it from pricing.
  • Narrow “A vs B” pages can do both. Check both tabs before you decide one is weak.

A comparison page with a few dozen visitors and one sale will look like a winner. Give low-traffic pages a longer date range until they have a few hundred visitors. See what revenue per visitor is.

When it has something to show, the Almost paid card lists people who reached pricing or checkout and left without paying, grouped by their last page. A comparison page that keeps appearing there may be making the other option look better than you meant it to.

Which search sends the buyers

Click Organic search on the Channels card and every card filters to it: the Landing page tab then shows which comparison pages earn from search, and the Source tab shows which search engine sent the visitors. Search engines leave the query out of organic visits, so you cannot see the exact words a buyer typed. The page they landed on is the best clue, since a page about Product A against Product B ranks for searches about that choice. See how channels are decided and organic search revenue tracking.

Ads are different. If you run search ads on alternatives searches and tag each ad link with utm_medium=cpc and utm_term, the visits count as Paid and the Keyword tab on the Channels card lists each term with its visitors and revenue. Without utm_medium, an ad click from a search engine looks like an organic one.

AI assistants answer questions such as “what is a good alternative to Product A” and link the pages they cite. Filter by the AI channel to see which comparison pages those answers send buyers to.

A worked example

The numbers below are made up, to show how to read the two tabs together. A SaaS with comparison pages under /compare/, over 60 days:

  • “Product A alternatives” lands 5,000 visitors, nearly all from Organic search, and 3 credited payments, with a red chip. It also shows up 6 times on the Almost paid card.
  • “Us vs Product A” lands 250 visitors and 1 payment, but it is the Before paying page for 9 payments whose visits started on the homepage or a blog post.
  • A search ad on “Product B alternative”, tagged with utm_term, sends 400 visitors to “Us vs Product B”; the Keyword tab credits it with 4 payments.

Read on its own, the “Us vs Product A” page looks weak as a landing page. Before paying shows it is one of the pages that closes, so it belongs one click from pricing. The alternatives page draws traffic that rarely pays and loses some at pricing, so reread it for claims that send people back to Product A, and link it to the narrower page. The paid keyword is earning; compare its revenue with what the ads cost. To weigh writing another comparison page against other work, try the SEO ROI calculator and see content marketing ROI.

When comparison page numbers look wrong

  • Revenue shows no landing page at all. Those payments were never matched to a visit. The line under the Overview numbers gives the matched share and the reasons for the rest; see Unattributed revenue.
  • A page buyers mention never gets credit. People who read a comparison weeks before buying may come back later through another channel, and the default model credits that later visit. Try first touch in Site settings and compare; see first touch or last touch.
  • Long evaluations fall outside the window. If buyers compare tools for months, raise the lookback from 90 to 365 days in Site settings.
  • Ad clicks land in Organic search. Tag ad links with utm_medium=cpc.

Frequently asked questions

Do comparison pages bring paying customers?

They often earn more per visitor than other content, because someone searching for a comparison is already choosing a tool. To know for your own site, rank your comparison pages against the rest by revenue per visitor, with each payment credited to the visit that brought the buyer.

How do I track revenue from alternatives pages?

Connect your payment provider and send the visitor ID with each checkout. Each payment is then joined to the visit before it, and the whole payment, after refunds, is credited to that visit's landing page, channel and referrer. In Virling, the Pages card lists alternatives pages under Landing page with their visitors, revenue and revenue per visitor.

Should I judge a comparison page as a landing page or as a page before paying?

Both. As a landing page it shows whether the page finds buyers, usually from search. Under Before paying it shows whether people who arrived elsewhere read it as the last step before they paid. A page can do one job well and the other badly.

Can I see which search terms brought buyers to a comparison page?

Not from organic search visits. Search engines send visitors without the query, so the landing page is the closest stand-in: a page about Product A against Product B ranked for a search about that choice. Filtering by Organic search and opening the Source tab shows which search engine sent them.

Can I see which ad keywords bring buyers to a comparison page?

Yes, if each ad link carries utm_medium=cpc and utm_term. The visits then count as Paid, and the Keyword tab on the Channels card lists each term with its visitors and revenue.

How many visitors does a comparison page need before I trust its numbers?

A few hundred visitors, and more than one or two sales. One sale on 40 visitors makes a page look far better than it is. Use a longer date range for comparison pages that get little traffic.
