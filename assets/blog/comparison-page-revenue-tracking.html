Do comparison pages bring paying customers? How to find out

By Devendra Variya, co-founder of Virling. Published 2026-11-21.

Comparison pages bring paying customers when the people who land on them, or read them just before buying, go on to pay. That is the only test that counts. Rankings and page views show that a “Product A vs Product B” page is found, not that it sells, and you only see the sales when each payment is credited to the visit and pages behind it.

This guide is for SaaS founders who have written, or are thinking of writing, “X vs Y” and “alternatives” pages and want to know whether they pay. It covers three views: the comparison page as the landing page, as the page seen before paying, and by the search source that sent its visitors. All the examples use placeholders such as Product A and Product B.

Why comparison pages are worth measuring on their own

  • The search shows intent. Someone who searches for “Product A alternatives” already has the problem and is choosing a tool. Someone who searches “what is” may never buy anything.
  • They do two jobs. A comparison page can find a buyer from search, or close a buyer who arrived elsewhere and wanted to check you against another option. The two jobs show up in different reports.
  • Traffic hides the difference. An alternatives page for a large rival can get many visitors who only browse, while a narrow “Product A vs Product B” page with little traffic brings the people who pay.

What you need first

Two links must exist before any page report means something. The payment provider must be connected, and the visitor ID must reach checkout, so a payment can be joined to the visit before it. See how to track Stripe revenue by traffic source and link checkout to visits.

Then it is a matter of which visit gets the credit. Virling describes a session by its first pageview: its channel, referrer, campaign and landing page. When a payment is matched, the whole payment, after refunds, is credited to one session in the lookback window, 90 days by default. The default model, last non-direct, picks the latest session that was not Direct, so a buyer who found your comparison page from Google and came back a week later by typing your address is still credited to Google and that page. See the attribution model.

How to read it in Virling

1. As landing page: does the page find buyers?

The Pages card on the dashboard has two tabs. Landing page lists the first page of the visit that was credited with the payment. Each row shows visitors, revenue and revenue per visitor, with a chip that compares the row with your site average: green above it, red below. Find your comparison and alternatives pages here and sort by revenue per visitor, not by visitors. If they share a path, such as /compare/ or /alternatives/, they are easy to pick out.

Wait until a page has a few hundred visitors before you trust its chip. One sale on 40 visitors makes a page look better than it is. See what revenue per visitor is.

2. As a page seen before paying: does the page close buyers?

The Before paying tab lists the last page viewed before the payment. A comparison page that shows up here is read by people who are about to buy, even when their visit started on the homepage or a blog post. If it rarely lands visitors but often comes right before payment, link it from pricing and from the pages that bring the most visitors.

The Almost paid card, which appears when it has something to show, lists visitors who reached pricing or checkout and did not pay, by their last page, with the revenue left on the table. A comparison page that keeps showing up there is worth rereading: something on it may be sending buyers away.

3. By search source: which search sends the buyers?

Every session gets one channel from its first pageview. A visit from a known search host such as google., bing.com or duckduckgo.com is Organic search; one from a known AI host such as chatgpt.com or perplexity.ai is AI. AI is checked before search, so gemini.google.com counts as AI. Ad clicks tagged with utm_medium=cpc count as Paid, even when they arrive from google.com. See how channels are decided.

Click Organic search on the Channels card and the whole page filters to it. The Landing page tab then shows which comparison pages earn from search, and the Source tab shows which search engine sent them, with every Google country domain on one google.com row. Do the same with AI: assistants often answer “what is a good alternative to Product A” and link the pages they cite. Click See visitors to open the people behind the number.

Search engines do not pass the search query, so you will not see which words a buyer typed. The landing page is the closest stand-in: a page about Product A against Product B ranked for a search about that choice. For the full search setup, see organic search revenue tracking.

A worked example

This is a hypothetical example with made-up numbers, to show the method. A SaaS with three comparison pages, over 90 days:

  • “Product A alternatives”: 6,000 landing visitors, 4 credited payments, a red chip. Most visitors come from Organic search.
  • “Product B vs us”: 700 landing visitors, 7 credited payments, a green chip, and the Before paying page for 5 more payments that landed elsewhere.
  • “Product C vs us”: 150 landing visitors, 1 payment. Too few to judge.

The alternatives page wins on traffic and loses on pay: it ranks for a broad search that draws browsers. Rewrite it around the buyers you want, and link to the narrower pages. The Product B page both finds and closes buyers, so link it from pricing and write the next page for the rival your customers name most often. The Product C page needs more time before it is judged. To decide whether writing more of these pages is worth the hours, put the numbers into the SEO ROI calculator, and see content marketing ROI for comparing them with the rest of your content.

What to check if the numbers look off

  • Most revenue has no landing page. The payment was not matched to a visit. The Matched revenue line under the Overview numbers gives the share matched and why the rest was not. See Unattributed revenue.
  • Comparison pages you expected to win are missing. By default the payment goes to the latest visit that was not Direct, which can be a later visit than the one that found the customer. Switch the model to first touch in Site settings to credit the earliest session, and compare. See first touch or last touch.
  • Old visits are not credited. Only sessions that started inside the lookback window count. If buyers read your comparison page months before paying, a 90-day window can miss them; you can choose 30, 60, 90 or 365 days in Site settings.
  • Ad clicks show as Organic search. An ad click that arrives from google.com with no utm_medium looks like an organic click. Tag ad links with utm_medium=cpc.

Frequently asked questions

Do comparison pages bring paying customers?

Often more per visitor than other content, because people searching for a comparison are already choosing. You only know for your site when each payment is credited to the landing page of the visit that brought the customer, and comparison pages are compared with the rest by revenue per visitor.

How do I track revenue from alternatives pages?

Connect your payment provider and pass the visitor ID into checkout. Each payment is then joined to the visit before it, and the whole payment, after refunds, is credited to that visit's landing page, channel and referrer. In Virling, the Pages card lists alternatives pages under Landing page with their visitors, revenue and revenue per visitor.

Should I judge a comparison page as a landing page or as a page before paying?

Both. As a landing page it shows whether the page finds buyers, usually from search. Under Before paying it shows whether people who arrived elsewhere read it as the last step before they paid. A page can do one job well and the other badly.

Can I see which search terms brought buyers to a comparison page?

Not from organic search visits. Search engines send visitors without the query, so the landing page is the closest stand-in: a page about Product A against Product B ranked for a search about that choice. Filtering by Organic search and opening the Source tab shows which search engine sent them.

How many visitors does a comparison page need before I trust its numbers?

A few hundred visitors, and more than one or two sales. One sale on 40 visitors makes a page look far better than it is. Use a longer date range for comparison pages that get little traffic.
