Expansion revenue by acquisition channel: which channels grow

By Devendra Variya, co-founder of Virling. Published 2026-11-26.

Expansion revenue by acquisition channel is the extra recurring revenue your existing customers add through upgrades, extra seats and add-ons, split by the marketing channel that first brought each customer. To get it, credit every customer to the channel of their first payment, keep that channel for every later payment, and add up what each channel's customers pay more than they did before. New MRR shows which channels win customers. Expansion shows which channels win customers who grow.

Two channels can bring the same new MRR this month and be worth very different amounts in a year, because the customers from one of them buy more seats and bigger plans while the others stay where they started.

What counts as expansion revenue

  • Upgrades. A move to a higher plan adds the difference: from $50 to $80 a month is $30 of expansion MRR.
  • Seats. A team that goes from 3 seats to 5 on a per-seat plan pays for 2 more.
  • Add-ons. A recurring extra, such as more usage or a module, added on top of the plan.
  • Not expansion. New customers are new MRR, even when they joined a team that already pays. A customer who left before the period and comes back is a win-back, not expansion. One-off purchases add revenue, but not recurring revenue.

Downgrades are the opposite, contraction, and cancellations are churned MRR. Net revenue retention puts all three together: NRR = (starting MRR + expansion - contraction - churned MRR) / starting MRR. The NRR calculator does the sum and lists the edge cases, such as monthly to annual moves.

Why the first channel matters more than the channel of the upgrade

An upgrade rarely starts with a marketing visit. The customer hits a limit inside the product, a teammate asks for a seat, or a billing email reminds them of a plan. If you credit the upgrade to the visit just before it, almost all expansion lands in Direct, in email or in your own app, and the channel that found the customer gets nothing for it.

The upgrade happened because the customer fit the product well enough to need more of it. That fit was decided by where they came from: what they searched for, which post they read, what they were promised. So the useful question is not "what made them click upgrade" but "which channels bring customers who later upgrade".

Virling uses one rule for every payment after the first. Any payment after a customer's first one takes the attribution of that first payment: its channel, referrer, campaign, landing page, country and device. A charge for an upgrade or another plan change is one of those payments, and so is a later one-time order by the same customer. A customer who came from a search two years ago and upgrades today adds that upgrade to organic search.

How upgrades in Stripe, Paddle and Polar reach Virling

Virling reads payments from each provider with a read-only key and matches them to the customer's first payment by the provider's customer ID. An upgrade arrives as a payment, and it is marked as a renewal when it is not the customer's first payment, or when the provider labels it as one:

  • Stripe. An invoice with billing reason subscription_update is a plan change, and subscription_cycle is a normal renewal. Any later charge of the same customer, such as a separate invoice for an add-on, keeps the first channel too. An invoice marked as paid outside Stripe has no charge and is not imported.
  • Paddle. A transaction with origin subscription_update, subscription_charge or subscription_recurring, or any later transaction of the same customer.
  • Polar. An order with billing reason subscription_update or subscription_cycle, or any later order by the same customer.

The upgrade payment carries no visitor ID of its own, and needs none: the first payment does the work. That is also why some expansion stays Unattributed:

  • The first payment was not attributed. Checkout did not carry the visitor ID, so the first payment has no channel to copy. See link checkout to visits.
  • The first payment is older than the import. Virling reads the last 90 days of payments on the first run. For a customer who started earlier, the earliest payment in Virling stands in as the first, and it rarely has a visit, so their upgrades are usually Unattributed.
  • No customer ID. A payment without one cannot be linked to a first payment.
  • A new customer ID or a new provider. Customers are matched per provider, so a customer who moved from one provider to another counts as two, and the second starts without the first channel.

Expansion by channel therefore fills in over time. Customers who arrive after you connect, with the visitor ID passed to checkout, carry their channel into every upgrade.

Lost MRR is contraction plus churned MRR. NRR leaves new MRR out, because it measures only the customers a channel already had.

By new MRR, communities win with more than half of it. By expansion, they are last: their customers added 2.5% of their starting MRR while email customers added 12%. Communities lost $600 and grew $100, so their NRR is 87.5%, and the $1,200 of new MRR mostly refills what leaves. Search and email both keep more than 100%: their existing customers pay more at the end of the month than at the start, before any new sale.

The blended 98.5% describes none of the three. It hides one channel that shrinks and two that grow, which call for different moves: fix what the community posts promise, and find more of whatever brings email and search customers.

How to read expansion alongside churn

  • Look at gross retention too. GRR is NRR without expansion. In the example it is 85% for communities, 97% for search and 95% for email. Email loses a little more than search and still has the higher NRR, because it expands more. A high NRR can hide churn, so read both.
  • Expansion needs customers who stay. A customer who cancels in the first month never gets to upgrade. A channel with high churn will usually show low expansion too, so fix the churn first; see churn by acquisition channel.
  • Count the customers behind it. Email's $240 could be one team adding seats. With small channels, one upgrade moves the whole row, so add several months together before you move budget.
  • Compare customers of the same age. New customers have had little time to upgrade. A channel you started last quarter holds mostly young customers, so its expansion looks low without being low.
  • Annual plans move slowly. Upgrades on annual plans often wait for the renewal, so a channel that sells mostly annual plans can show little expansion for months.

For new and churned MRR per channel, read MRR by acquisition channel. For how renewals keep the first channel, read subscription renewal attribution.

Frequently asked questions

What is expansion revenue by acquisition channel?

It is the extra recurring revenue existing customers add through upgrades, seats and add-ons, split by the marketing channel that brought each customer. Each customer stays in the channel credited for their first payment, so an upgrade years later still counts for the channel that won them.

Which channel gets the credit for an upgrade?

In Virling, the channel of the customer's first payment. An upgrade charge has no visit of its own, so Virling copies the channel, referrer and campaign of the first payment, matched by the provider's customer ID.

Why are some upgrades Unattributed?

Usually because the customer's first payment was not attributed, or is older than the 90-day import. Payments without a customer ID cannot be linked to a first payment, and a customer who moved to another provider counts as a new customer.

How do I calculate net revenue retention by channel?

Take the MRR a channel's existing customers paid at the start of the period, add their expansion, subtract contraction and churned MRR, and divide by the starting MRR. Leave out new customers who joined during the period.

Should I judge a channel by new MRR or by expansion?

Read both, with churn. New MRR shows what a channel wins this month. Expansion and net revenue retention show whether those customers grow or shrink after that. A channel that leads on new MRR can still lose money over a year if its customers leave and never upgrade.
