Which landing page brings the most revenue? Landing page revenue tracking

By Devendra Variya, co-founder of Virling. Published 2026-11-22.

The landing page that brings the most revenue is the one whose visitors pay you the most, and you only see it when each payment is credited to the page the customer entered on. Rank landing pages by the revenue of the visitors who entered on them, and by revenue per visitor, not by conversion rate alone. A page can convert well into small orders and earn less than a page that converts fewer visitors into large ones.

This guide is for founders and store owners who run several landing pages, from ad pages and product pages to blog posts and comparison pages, and want to know which ones earn. It covers what to track, how to read entry page against page before paying, and how to avoid trusting a page on too little data.

Why conversion rate alone misleads

  • It counts buyers, not money. A page that sells the cheapest plan to 3% of visitors can earn less than a page that sells the yearly plan to 1%.
  • It ignores refunds. A page that promises too much can convert well and then lose the money back.
  • Many buyers do not pay on the first visit. Many people land on a page, leave, and pay days later after typing your address. Without a record that ties the payment to the visit that found them, that sale lands in Direct and the landing page gets no credit.

What you need first

Two links must exist before any landing page report means something. The payment provider must be connected, and the visitor ID must reach checkout, so a payment can be joined to the visit before it. See how to track Stripe revenue by traffic source and link checkout to visits.

Then it is a matter of which visit gets the credit. Virling describes a session by its first pageview: its channel, referrer, campaign and landing page. When a payment is matched, the whole payment, after refunds, is credited to that session’s channel, referrer, campaign and landing page. See the attribution model.

Entry page versus page before paying

Two pages matter for every sale, and they answer different questions.

  • Entry page (landing page): the first page of the visit that is credited with the payment. It tells you which pages bring buyers in.
  • Page before paying: the last page viewed before the payment, often pricing, a plan page or checkout. It tells you which pages close the sale.

A blog post or ad page can be a strong entry page and never appear before paying, while pricing appears before almost every payment and rarely lands anyone. Judge each page on the job it does. Reading only the last page credits pricing for every sale; reading only the entry page hides the pages that close.

Revenue per visitor per page

Revenue per visitor is the revenue credited to a page divided by the visitors who entered on it. It combines conversion rate and order value in one number, so it is the fairest way to compare a busy page with a quiet one. See what revenue per visitor is, or work it out for any page with the revenue per visitor calculator.

Look at total revenue too. A page with high revenue per visitor and very few visitors may be worth sending more traffic to, but it does not yet bring the most money.

How to read it in Virling

1. Open the Pages card

The Pages card on the dashboard has two tabs. Landing page lists the first page of the visit. Before paying lists the last page before paying. Each row shows visitors, revenue and revenue per visitor, with a chip that compares the row with your site average: green above it, red below. Pick a range with the date picker at the top right; each number shows its change against the previous period of the same length.

2. Rank by revenue, then by revenue per visitor

Revenue tells you which landing page brings the most money now. Revenue per visitor tells you which one would bring the most if it had more visitors. A row with many visitors and a red chip is loud but does not pay. A small row with a green chip is worth more of your time. Wait until a page has a few hundred visitors before you trust its chip: one sale on 40 visitors makes a page look better than it is.

3. Filter by channel and campaign

Click a row to filter the whole page by it, and the Channels card shows which channels and campaigns send that page’s visitors. Or click a channel first, such as Paid, and the Landing page tab shows which pages earn from it. Click See visitors to open the people behind the number.

4. Check the pages around the sale

The Almost paid card, which appears when it has something to show, lists visitors who reached pricing or checkout and did not pay, by channel or by their last page, with the revenue left on the table. The Reading card shows how long each page stays visible and how far it is scrolled, for every visitor or only for buyers. The Page speed card shows load time for your most loaded pages next to the conversion rate and revenue of the visitors who loaded them. To see which step between the page and the payment loses the most people, use the funnel conversion calculator.

5. Compare before and after a change

After you rewrite a landing page, use Compare to other dates in the date picker to set the weeks after the change against the weeks before, and read the page’s row on the Landing page tab. To check whether a difference between two pages or two versions is more than chance, put their visitors, conversions and revenue into the A/B test calculator.

A worked example

Made-up numbers to show the method. A store with three landing pages, over 90 days:

  • An ad landing page: 4,000 visitors, 3.0% conversion rate, $40 average order. $4,800 revenue, $1.20 per visitor.
  • A product guide: 2,500 visitors, 1.2% conversion rate, $180 average order. $5,400 revenue, $2.16 per visitor.
  • A gift page: 300 visitors, 2 sales. Too few sales to judge.

The ad page wins on conversion rate and loses on revenue. The guide brings the most money and the most per visitor, so it deserves more traffic and a closer look at what its readers buy. The gift page needs more visitors before it is judged.

What to check if the numbers look off

  • Most revenue has no landing page. The payment was not matched to a visit. The Matched revenue line under the Overview numbers gives the share matched and why the rest was not. See Unattributed revenue.
  • Pages you expected to win are missing. By default the payment is credited to the latest visit that was not Direct, which can be a later visit than the one that found the customer. Switch the attribution model to first touch in Site settings to credit the earliest session, and compare. See first touch or last touch.
  • A page with traffic shows no revenue. Revenue counts on the payment date, so a page whose visitors pay days later needs a longer range.

Frequently asked questions

Which landing page brings the most revenue?

The one whose visitors bring the most revenue, after refunds. To see it, credit each payment to the first page of the visit that brought the customer, then compare pages by revenue and by revenue per visitor, not by conversion rate or visitors.

How do I track revenue by landing page?

Connect your payment provider and pass the visitor ID into checkout. The payment is then joined to the visit before it, and the whole payment, after refunds, is credited to that visit's landing page, channel and campaign. Virling shows this on the Pages card, under Landing page.

What is the difference between the entry page and the page before paying?

The entry page, or landing page, is the first page of the visit that is credited with the payment. The page before paying is the last page viewed before the payment, often pricing or checkout. The entry page shows what brings buyers in; the page before paying shows what closes them.

Why not rank landing pages by conversion rate?

Conversion rate counts buyers, not what they pay. A page that converts 3% of visitors into a cheap plan can earn less than one that converts 1% into a yearly plan. Revenue per visitor takes both the rate and the order value into account.

How many visitors does a landing page need before I trust its numbers?

Wait for a few hundred visitors and several sales. One sale on 40 visitors makes a page look better than it is. To check whether two pages or two versions really differ, put their visitors, conversions and revenue into an A/B test calculator.
