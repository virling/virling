Which blog posts bring paying customers? How to find out

By Devendra Variya, co-founder of Virling. Published 2026-11-02.

The blog posts that bring paying customers are the ones whose readers later pay you, and you only see them when each payment is credited to the first page of the visit that brought the customer. Page views cannot show this. A post can be read by thousands and bring nobody who buys, while a quiet post brings the people who do.

This guide shows how to credit payments to landing pages, which numbers to compare, and how to read the result without over-trusting it.

Why page views mislead

  • Reach is not intent. A post that ranks for a broad question draws many readers who will never need your product.
  • The buyer may not read the post you expect. The pages that get the most views are often the pages that matter least for revenue.
  • Posts pay late. A reader finds a post, leaves, and buys weeks later after typing your address. A post earns over months, not on the day it is read.

To credit a post with that later payment, Virling joins the payment to the reader’s earlier visit through the visitor ID passed into checkout, and the post that opened the visit gets the revenue. Which visit that is depends on the attribution model.

Find your posts on the Pages card

The landing page guide walks through the Pages card step by step. For posts, three moves are enough:

  • On the Landing page tab, read revenue per visitor for each post, not visitors. See what revenue per visitor is.
  • Click a post to filter the dashboard by it and see which channel sends its readers. A post that earns from organic search is a different asset from one that earned it from a single Reddit thread. A short burst of Google visitors can come from the Discover feed; see how to tell Discover visits from search.
  • Give a post at least a few hundred readers before you judge it.

How long a post takes to pay back

A post costs money once, when it is written, and earns for as long as it ranks or gets shared. Its payback time is how many months of credited revenue it takes to cover that cost. Set the date range to each month since the post went live and note its revenue on the Landing page tab; when the running total passes what you paid for the post, it has paid back.

Two things stretch that time. A new post needs weeks to rank, and its readers need time to decide: the time to pay guide shows how to read the gap between first visit and payment for each channel. Judge a post only once its readers have had that long. Reading depth: is the post read by people who buy?

The Reading card shows, for each page, how long it stays visible and how far it is scrolled, for every visitor or only for buyers. Switch between the two for the same post.

  • Buyers scroll to the end, everyone else leaves at the top. The end of the post is doing the work. Move its point, or its link to the product, higher.
  • Buyers and everyone else both stop early. The post may bring buyers for its title, not its text. Shorten it or put the answer first.
  • Long visible time, few buyers. Readers use the post as a reference. It may build trust, but check that it links to the product at all.

Posts that start journeys and posts read before paying

Some posts are the first thing a future customer reads. Others are read in the last visit, right before checkout. Both earn, in different ways.

  • Posts that start journeys show up when the payment is credited to the earliest visit. By default Virling credits the latest visit that was not Direct, so switch the attribution model to first touch in Site settings and read the Landing page tab again. Posts that gain revenue under first touch are where customers first meet you. See first touch or last touch.
  • Posts read right before paying show up on the Before paying tab. A how-to or comparison post here answers the last question a buyer has. Link it from pricing and from your onboarding emails.

To see the whole path for one customer, open the Visitors page, filter to visitors who paid and search for the post. Each visitor page lists every visit in order, with the payment where it happened.

Which topics to write more of

Virling lists each post as its own row and does not group posts by topic, so tag them yourself: a spreadsheet with each post’s URL and topic is enough. Then add up visitors and credited revenue per topic over a long range, such as 12 months, and divide to get revenue per visitor for each topic.

A topic with high revenue per visitor and few posts is the one to write more of. A topic with plenty of traffic and little revenue may still be worth keeping if its posts start journeys under first touch, but it should not get the next post by default. Put topics in a URL folder, such as /blog/guides/, and the grouping is easier to read later.

A worked example

Made-up numbers to show the method. Over 90 days:

  • A comparison post: 900 visitors, 6 sales, high revenue per visitor.
  • A “what is” explainer: 14,000 visitors, 5 sales, low revenue per visitor.
  • A tutorial: 2,100 visitors, 1 sale. Too few sales to judge.

The explainer wins on traffic and loses on pay. The comparison post deserves a sister post on the next rival. The tutorial needs more time, or a clearer step to the product, before it is judged. Do not delete the explainer before checking it under first touch: it may be where people first meet you.

When a post shows no revenue

  • It is new. Readers have not had time to buy. Wait out your usual time to pay.
  • Its readers come back through another page. Under the default model a later visit can take the credit. Check it under first touch.
  • No payment has a page at all. The visitor ID is not reaching checkout. Unattributed revenue lists the usual causes.

Frequently asked questions

Which blog posts bring paying customers?

The ones whose visitors later pay. To see them, credit each payment to the landing page of the visit that brought the customer, then compare pages by revenue per visitor, not by page views.

Which posts start customer journeys and which close them?

Switch the attribution model to first touch and read the Landing page tab to see the posts customers read first. The Before paying tab shows the pages read in the last visit before the payment, often a how-to or comparison post next to pricing.

Should I delete blog posts that bring no revenue?

Not on that evidence alone. A post can bring no payments because it is new, because it gets few visitors, or because it reaches readers who buy later through another page. Check visitors, revenue per visitor and time to pay before you change or remove anything.

Is a post with a lot of traffic and no revenue a problem?

It is a question, not a verdict. Look at who the post attracts and what it offers next. A post that draws readers who are not your buyers can be rewritten for a different search, or linked to the product more clearly.

How long does a blog post take to pay for itself?

Add up the revenue credited to the post month by month since it went live, and note the month the total passes what the post cost to write. New posts need time to rank and their readers need time to buy, so judge a post only after your usual time to pay has passed.
