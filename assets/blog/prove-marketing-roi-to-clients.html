How to prove marketing ROI to clients: a monthly method for agencies

By Devendra Variya, co-founder of Virling. Published 2026-11-18, updated 2026-11-25.

To prove marketing ROI to a client, show them money they were actually paid, split by the channel that brought each buyer, next to what each channel cost. Clicks, impressions and platform conversions are not proof: the client can check none of them against their bank. Their payment provider they can check.

This post walks through the method in four steps: tag every link, connect the client's payment provider, report revenue by channel every month, and be honest about the revenue you cannot credit.

Why traffic reports do not prove ROI

A report full of sessions and click-through rates answers "did people see it?". The client is asking "did it pay?". Ad platforms report their own conversions, each counted its own way, and the totals of several platforms often add up to more than the client was paid. A client who compares that to their revenue stops trusting the whole report.

The fix is to start from the other end: take the payments, then work back to the visit before each one.

Step 1: tag every link you control

Every link your agency places, in an ad, a newsletter, a social post or a partner page, should carry utm_source, utm_medium and utm_campaign. Untagged clicks from email and many apps arrive with no referrer and land in Direct, where nobody gets credit for them.

  • Use one naming convention across every client, so "newsletter" never turns up as "Newsletter" or "email-nl" in the same report. The UTM naming template gives you one.
  • Agree the campaign names with the client before launch, so the names in the report are ones they recognise.
  • Keep a list of every tagged link per client. It is the first thing to check when a channel looks wrong.

More on the tags themselves is in UTM parameters for revenue tracking.

Step 2: connect the client's payment provider

Revenue belongs to the payment provider, not the ad platform. Ask the client for a read-only key from Stripe, Paddle, Shopify or whatever they sell through, so you can read payments without being able to change anything.

Then link each payment to a visit. The site's analytics gives each visitor an ID, and the checkout passes that ID along with the payment, usually as checkout metadata. With the ID on the payment, you can find that visitor's visits before they paid and credit the payment to a channel under one fixed rule, such as the last non-direct visit within 30 to 90 days. Pick the rule once and keep it, so months compare fairly. The trade-offs are in first-touch or last-touch attribution.

Step 3: report revenue by channel every month

Send the same one-page report on the same day each month. For each channel, show:

  • Revenue credited to it, and the same figure for last month
  • Paying customers, so one large order does not hide a channel that brought few buyers
  • Spend: everything the channel cost, including ad bills, tools, freelancers and your team's hours
  • ROI: gross profit minus spend, divided by spend

Use gross profit, not revenue, for ROI: revenue alone makes every channel look better than it is. The marketing ROI calculator does the sums per channel, and the client report template lays out revenue by channel, the change versus last month, top campaigns and next steps on one page you can print or send.

End with two or three next steps, each tied to a row in the table: move budget toward the channel that grew, fix the campaign that fell. That is the part the client acts on.

A paragraph to send with it, using the same made-up numbers:

"This month we spent $5,650 and you gained 85 new paying customers, worth $4,250 in their first month. Email paid for itself within the month. Organic search pays back in 2.0 months and paid ads in 2.9, if those customers keep paying. Social brought 4 customers for $900 and would take 6.4 months, so we suggest halving that budget next month and putting the rest into a second email campaign. $300 of the revenue, or 7%, came from payments we could not link to a visit, and we are checking the checkout for missing visitor IDs. You can check the total against your payment provider."

Step 4: explain Unattributed honestly

Some payments cannot be credited to any visit. Put them in the report as their own row, called Unattributed, rather than spreading them across channels or leaving them out. Common reasons:

  • The checkout did not carry a visitor ID, often because a step in the setup was missed
  • The visitor blocked the analytics script, or the visit happened on a page without it
  • A payment link was opened from an email, a QR code or another site, with no visit before it
  • The visit was outside the lookback window, or the payment was made before tracking started

Say what share of revenue is Unattributed and what you are doing about it. Total revenue still matches what the client was paid, and a client who sees the gap named trusts the rest of the table more. If the share falls month after month, that is progress worth reporting too. The full list of causes is in the Unattributed docs.

Frequently asked questions

How do I prove marketing ROI to a client?

Credit each of the client's real payments to the channel of the visit before it, then show revenue and paying customers per channel next to what each channel cost. Numbers taken from the client's payment provider can be checked against their bank; platform conversions cannot.

How do you calculate marketing ROI for a client?

Take the revenue credited to a channel, multiply by the client's gross margin, subtract everything the channel cost, and divide by that cost. An ROI of 0% means the channel exactly paid for itself.

Why not use the conversions the ad platforms report?

Each platform counts its own conversions in its own way, and a buyer who saw two platforms can be counted by both. Their totals often add up to more than the client was paid. Starting from the client's payments avoids that.

What should I say about Unattributed revenue?

Show it as its own row, give its share of revenue, and say why it happens: checkouts without a visitor ID, blocked scripts, payment links opened from email, or payments made before tracking started. Then say what you are doing to shrink it.

How often should I report ROI to clients?

Monthly for the full report with spend and ROI, so each channel has time to show results. A short weekly update on revenue by channel helps catch a channel that drops before the monthly call.

How do you calculate payback for a client's channel?

Divide what the channel cost in the month by the monthly gross profit from the new customers it brought. As a made-up example, $3,000 of spend that brings $1,050 of gross profit a month pays back in 2.9 months, if those customers keep paying. For a client whose customers buy once, report ROI instead.
